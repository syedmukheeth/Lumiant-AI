# Inventory levers: cash, cost and service

How inventory decisions turn into cash, cost and service. These are general practices to agree with the organisation's planners and finance team; Lumiant only calculates the figures behind them from the data the organisation has connected.

## Working capital tied up in stock
Stock on the shelf is cash the business has already spent and cannot use for anything else. Inventory is often one of the largest parts of working capital, so holding less of it frees cash without cutting sales. The main levers are buying only what will be used, using up or selling surplus stock, shortening supplier lead times so less stock sits in the pipeline, and setting reorder levels and lot sizes from real demand. Reducing stock that customers need is not a saving: a stock-out costs sales and goodwill, so the aim is the right stock, not the least.

## Freeing cash from inventory: where to start
A practical order of work to free up cash: first see where the value sits, by warehouse, item group and ABC class, because a small share of items usually holds most of it. Then look at items above their maximum stock and stop or delay open purchase orders for them. Next review stock that has had no issues for many months, and lots that will expire soon. Finally tune the reorder levels and order quantities that caused the surplus, so it does not come back. Each step needs different data: value needs valuation prices, the maximum needs a reorder policy, idle stock needs movement history and expiry needs lots with expiry dates. Asked about cash or cost, the Analyst sizes these levers together from whatever data is connected.

## Carrying cost
Carrying cost, also called holding cost, is what it costs each year to keep stock: the cost of the capital tied up, storage space and handling, insurance, loss and damage, and the risk that items expire or become obsolete. Many companies estimate it at 15 to 30 percent of inventory value per year, but the right rate is a finance decision for each organisation. Lumiant holds no carrying-cost rate; once the organisation has chosen one, the yearly carrying cost of a stock position is its value multiplied by that rate. Lowering average stock, clearing idle items and avoiding expiry write-offs reduce carrying cost directly.

## Inventory turns and days of cover
Inventory turns tell how many times a year the stock is used up and replaced: the yearly value of goods issued divided by the average inventory value. Days of cover tell how long the current stock would last at the recent rate of use: quantity on hand divided by average daily issues. Few turns or many days of cover point to stock that ties up cash; very few days of cover point to a risk of running out. Both need stock movement history as well as stock on hand, and turns in value also need valuation prices. Compare them within an item group or stage, because spares and raw materials naturally turn at very different speeds.

## Options for disposing of excess stock
When excess stock is more than will be used in a reasonable time, the options, roughly from most to least value recovered, are: use it elsewhere in the business (transfer it to a warehouse or plant that needs it, or use it as an approved alternate), return it to the supplier, perhaps for a restocking fee, sell it to another customer or a surplus dealer, rework it into an item that sells, and finally scrap or donate it and write it off. Stop new purchases of the item first, otherwise the surplus comes back. Disposal is a decision for people: Lumiant can show which items are above maximum, how long they have been idle and what they are worth, but it never posts a transfer, return or write-off.

## Clearing idle and obsolete stock
Stock with no issues for a long time, and items that will never be used again, are where carrying cost buys nothing. Review them in a fixed order: confirm the item really has no demand (open orders, bills of materials, planned maintenance), check whether it is a critical spare kept on purpose, check whether a newer item replaces it, and only then choose to transfer, return, sell or write it off. Give each decision an owner and a date, and block new purchases of items marked for run-down. This review needs stock movement history; lifecycle status, BOM use and open demand make it safer where they are mapped.

## FEFO and expiry
First expired, first out (FEFO) means issuing the lot that expires soonest first, rather than the lot received first (FIFO). For food, chemicals, adhesives, rubber, medicines and other items with a shelf life, FEFO is the main defence against write-offs. Good practice is to record an expiry date on every lot at receipt, review the lots that expire within the next one to three months, move them to where they will be used, and stop ordering more of an item while short-dated stock is on hand. Expired stock should be blocked so nobody issues it by mistake. Lumiant needs lots with expiry dates to show expiring and expired stock.

## Safety stock and service level
Safety stock is the extra quantity kept to cover surprises in demand and in supplier lead time. The service level is the chance of not running out during one replenishment cycle; raising it from 95 to 99 percent usually needs much more safety stock, so the target should differ by item: high for critical and A items, lower for cheap or easily replaced ones. A common method sets safety stock from how much demand varies over the lead time, multiplied by a factor for the chosen service level. Lumiant does not set safety stock; it reads the safety stock and reorder levels the organisation records and shows where stock falls below them.

## Tuning reorder levels and order quantities
A reorder level should cover the expected use during the supplier lead time plus the safety stock, and a maximum is roughly the reorder level plus a sensible order quantity. Levels set once and never reviewed drift away from reality: items whose use has dropped carry too much, and items whose use has grown run short. Review them regularly, starting with the items most often short and those most often above maximum. Order quantities balance ordering effort against holding cost, within the supplier's minimum order quantity and order multiple. Lumiant shows items below reorder level, above maximum or without any policy; it never changes the policy in the source system.

## Supplier lead time and reliability
Longer and less reliable lead times force more stock: the reorder level must cover use during the lead time, and safety stock must cover how much it varies. Cutting a lead time by a week removes about a week of average use from the stock that is needed. Measure lead time from purchase order to goods receipt, compare each supplier's promised dates with actual receipts, and agree improvements, shorter lead times or consignment stock with the suppliers that matter most. Lumiant reads planned lead times from item, plant or supplier data and actual order and receipt dates from purchase records.

## How data quality affects valuation
Inventory value is only as good as the data behind it. Stock without a valuation price is left out, so the total is understated; a wrong price unit or unit of measure can overstate a line many times over; stock in quality inspection, blocked or in transit may or may not be counted, depending on the source report; and duplicate item codes split one part's stock and demand across several records. Before acting on a valuation, check the data quality findings: missing prices, missing units, items without a policy, and differences between the value the source system reports and quantity times price. Lumiant flags these gaps instead of guessing a value.

## What a profit analysis needs
Profit and margin come from sales, not from stock. A profit analysis needs sales or invoice history (what was sold, when and to whom), selling prices and discounts, and the cost of the goods sold. Inventory data alone gives quantities, valuation prices and movements: it can show what stock is worth and how fast it moves, but not revenue or profit. Customer order lines with selling prices and margins, where an organisation maps them, cover open orders only, not completed sales. To answer profit questions, connect sales or finance data first; until then, Lumiant can help with the inventory side of profit: working capital, carrying cost, and excess, idle and expiring stock.
